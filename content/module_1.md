# Lesson 1.1: What AI Actually Is — In Plain Language

## What You'll Learn
- How AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude actually work — no engineering degree, no patience required
- Why AI crushes certain business tasks and completely falls apart at others
- The 80/20 rule that separates business owners who benefit from AI from those who end up talking to themselves

---

Here's something that happened to me early on.

I asked ChatGPT to write a client email — the kind I'd been writing variations of for 30 years. It came back in about eight seconds. It was good. Like, *annoyingly* good. I sat there for a moment, genuinely unsure whether to be impressed or insulted.

That moment is when I realized I'd been thinking about this whole thing wrong.

### What's Actually Going On Under the Hood

When you type a question into ChatGPT or Claude, it feels like you're talking to something intelligent. The response comes back polished, confident, and often genuinely useful. Your brain wants to believe there's a mind in there somewhere.

There isn't. And here's the thing — understanding that isn't a letdown. It's your biggest advantage.

AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude are called large language models, or LLMs. That's a technical term for a very sophisticated pattern-matching system. When you type a question, the AI predicts the most likely next word, then the next, then the next — thousands of times in a row — until it's assembled a complete response.

Think of it as the world's most well-read autocomplete. You know how your phone suggests the next word when you're texting? Same fundamental idea, scaled up by billions of examples from books, articles, websites, and documents across the entire internet.

The AI doesn't understand your question. It doesn't think about it. It calculates the statistically most probable sequence of words that should follow your input. That's it.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "The first time I understood that AI was just very sophisticated autocomplete, everything clicked. It explained why it's so good at writing emails and so bad at giving me advice about my actual business. It doesn't know my business. It knows what words usually follow other words."

### Why This Matters for Your Business

This distinction — pattern-matching versus actual thinking — has real consequences for how you should (and shouldn't) use AI.

**What AI is excellent at:**
- Drafting emails, proposals, and standard business correspondence
- Summarizing long documents or reports
- Reformatting and organizing information you already have
- Generating first drafts of marketing copy, job descriptions, or policies
- Answering well-documented questions (tax filing deadlines, standard business procedures)
- Translating between languages or adjusting tone and reading level

**What AI is terrible at:**
- Making judgment calls about your specific business situation
- Providing reliable financial or legal advice
- Understanding context it hasn't been told — like your company culture, your client relationships, or the unspoken dynamics in your industry
- Knowing when its own information is outdated or flat wrong
- Understanding risk the way you do after decades in business

I've used AI daily for over three years across multiple businesses. The pattern never changes: AI handles the repetitive, language-heavy work quickly and well. The moment you need judgment, experience, or knowledge of your actual situation — that's where you're the one who matters.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** AI drafts. You decide. That's not a workaround — that's the model. The moment you let AI make the judgment call and you just sign off, you've handed over the part that makes your business *your* business.

### The Fear Problem

Most fear around AI comes from treating it like it's intelligent. Headlines scream about AI "taking over" or "replacing" entire professions. That sells clicks. It doesn't reflect reality.

If you've been in business for 20 or 30 years, you've seen technology panics before. Spreadsheets were going to eliminate accountants. Email was going to eliminate mail carriers. E-commerce was going to eliminate retail. In each case, the technology changed the work — and the people who understood the fundamentals adapted, and often came out ahead.

AI is the same story. It's a powerful tool that changes how you do certain tasks. It doesn't replace the judgment, relationships, and domain expertise that actually make your business work.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "I was a skeptic. Took me longer than it should have to sit down and actually try it. My honest regret isn't that I eventually started using AI — it's that I wasted two years being suspicious of it while other people were getting a quiet head start."

### The 80/20 Rule of AI

Here's the framework I use every single day: AI gets you 80% of the way there. Your expertise provides the critical 20%.

That first 80% is where AI saves you enormous amounts of time. A client email that used to take 15 minutes? AI produces a solid draft in 30 seconds. A job description? Comprehensive starting point in under a minute. Summarizing a 40-page contract for a quick review? Done in seconds.

But that last 20% — the part where you adjust the email's tone because you know *this particular client* is sensitive about pricing, or you add the specific qualification to the job description because your last hire without it was a disaster, or you flag the contract clause that AI didn't recognize as unusual for your industry — that's where your decades of experience earn their keep.

The business owners who get the most from AI understand this split. They don't fight it. They use AI to eliminate the tedious 80% and spend their time on the high-value 20% where their expertise actually makes a difference.

> **👉 AI Ron Recommends:** Every time you get a solid AI draft, resist the urge to send it as-is. Read it once and ask yourself: "What does this draft not know about my situation?" The answer to that question is almost always where your real value goes in. Takes 60 seconds. Worth every one of them.

A typical business owner who adopts this 80/20 approach reports saving 5-10 hours per week on routine communication, document drafting, and information processing. That's not a hypothetical number. That's what I've experienced, and it's consistent with what I hear from other business owners making this shift.

### What You Don't Need to Know

You don't need to understand neural networks, transformer architecture, or how models are trained. That's like saying you need to understand internal combustion to drive a car. You need to know what the tool does well, what it does poorly, and how to use it effectively. That's what this course teaches.

## Your Practical Task

**Step 1:** Open ChatGPT (chat.openai.com) or Claude (claude.ai). If you don't have an account, create a free one — two minutes, tops.

**Step 2:** Type this exact prompt:

Read that response. It will sound confident and reasonable. Now ask yourself: does this advice account for your specific cash flow situation? Your existing contracts? Your local market conditions? Your relationship with your bank?

It doesn't. It can't. Recognizing that gap is the single most important thing you'll take from this entire course.

**1. ChatGPT (by OpenAI)**
The most widely known AI tool and the one most people start with. ChatGPT is a strong general-purpose assistant.

My personal daily workhorse. Claude tends to produce more nuanced, well-structured writing and is notably strong at following complex instructions.

Less likely to overstate its confidence than some alternatives. Excellent at maintaining a consistent voice and tone — important if you want your writing to actually sound like you.

*Where it falls short:* Smaller ecosystem than ChatGPT — fewer integrations and add-ons. No native image generation. Can occasionally hedge too much and qualify everything to death when you just want a direct answer.

*Where it falls short:* Not designed for long-form writing, creative work, or complex document processing. It's a research tool, not a general assistant. Answers tend to be shorter and more factual than what you'd get from ChatGPT or Claude.

*Pricing (as of early 2026 — verify at perplexity.ai):* Free tier is generous for basic research. Pro is $20/month (roughly $17/month annually) for unlimited advanced searches — worthwhile if you do regular research.

### Free vs. Paid: When to Upgrade

Start free. Every one of these tools has a free tier that lets you accomplish real work. Here's when it makes sense to pay:

**Upgrade when you're hitting limits.** If you find yourself using the tool daily and running into message caps or slower response times, the paid tier removes those frustrations. Your time has a dollar value. If the free tier costs you 30 minutes of waiting and re-prompting per week, the $20/month pays for itself immediately.

**Upgrade when you need the better models.** The paid tiers give you access to more capable models that produce better output on complex tasks. If you're using AI for client-facing work — proposals, reports, marketing — the quality difference is noticeable and worth it.

**Don't upgrade "just in case."** If you're using a tool twice a week and the free tier works fine, save your money.

My recommendation: Start with free accounts on ChatGPT and Claude. Use both for a month. You'll naturally gravitate toward one. Upgrade that one to the paid tier. Add Perplexity if you do significant research. Consider Gemini's paid tier only if you're deep in Google Workspace.

Keep in mind: by the time you read this, pricing tiers may have shifted, new models may have launched, and feature sets may have reshuffled. That's normal in this space. The *strategy* — start free, upgrade what you actually use, avoid the tool trap — stays the same regardless of what the price tags say next month.

> **👉 AI Ron Recommends:** Don't subscribe to anything until you've used the free tier enough to know it's actually saving you time. One month is usually enough to know. Two weeks is too short — you're still in the learning curve. A month gives you real data.

### The Tool Trap

Here's a pattern I've watched play out more times than I can count.

A business owner gets excited about AI. Signs up for eight different tools. Six months later, they're less productive than when they started — spending more time switching between interfaces and watching tutorial videos than they save on actual work.

This is the tool trap. More tools doesn't mean more productivity.

The business owners who get the most from AI typically use one or two tools well. They learn the nuances — how to write better prompts, which tasks to delegate to AI, how to build repeatable workflows. Depth beats breadth every single time.

If someone tells you that you need ten AI subscriptions to stay competitive, they're selling subscriptions, not productivity.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** Pick one tool, use it until it becomes second nature, *then* add another if you identify a specific gap. Adding tools before you've mastered one is how you end up with eight browser tabs, three monthly charges, and not much to show for it.

Master the fundamentals first. Once you're consistently saving time with a general-purpose AI tool, you'll naturally identify specific bottlenecks in your business that a specialized tool could solve. That's when you evaluate specialized options — with a clear problem in mind, not because a newsletter said they were "essential for 2026."

# Lesson 1.3: Risk, Limits, and Sanity Checks

## What You'll Learn
- The three biggest risks of using AI in business — and specific habits that neutralize each one
- Exactly what you should and shouldn't share with AI tools, especially around client data
- A CPA-trained verification approach that takes 60 seconds and prevents expensive mistakes

---

Let me tell you about a law firm that made national news. Not for winning a big case. For submitting a legal brief full of citations — complete with case names, court dates, and ruling summaries — that were entirely made up. The attorney used AI to research the brief, the AI invented perfectly-formatted, totally fictional case law, and nobody caught it before it was filed.

The judge was not amused.

You and I are not going to do that. But here's the thing: the exact same mechanism that fabricated those court cases is operating every time you get a response from ChatGPT or Claude. You just need to know how to manage it.

### The Confidence Problem

AI's most dangerous feature isn't inaccuracy. It's confidence.

When AI gets something wrong, it doesn't hesitate, flag uncertainty, or say "I'm not sure about this one." It states the wrong information with the same polished, authoritative tone it uses for everything else. Research has found that nearly half of business users have made significant decisions based on AI-generated information that turned out to be fabricated.

These errors have a name: hallucinations. The AI produces text that looks right, reads well, and is completely made up. This isn't a bug that gets patched in the next update. It's a fundamental characteristic of how these tools work. Remember — they're predicting likely word sequences, not verifying facts.

Here are the types of hallucinations you'll actually encounter in business:

**Fabricated citations.** Ask AI to reference a study or statistic, and it may invent one — complete with author names, journal titles, and publication years that don't exist. See: the law firm above.

**Confident but outdated information.** AI models are trained on data up to a certain point. They may give you tax rates from two years ago, reference regulations that have since been updated, or describe business practices that changed. They won't tell you the information is stale. They don't know.

**Plausible but wrong specifics.** Ask about your industry's regulatory requirements in a specific state, and AI may give you an answer that sounds precise but mixes up jurisdictions, misapplies rules, or invents requirements that don't exist. Convincingly.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "The thing that gets people isn't when AI says something obviously wrong. It's when AI says something that is 90% right, stated with complete confidence, and the 10% that's wrong is the part that matters. That's the version that causes problems."

### The Verification Habit

The single most valuable habit in this entire course is verification. Not optional verification when you "have a feeling something might be off." Systematic verification, every time, on anything that matters.

Here's the approach I use. It takes about 60 seconds and comes from decades of CPA-trained thinking where you never trust a single source for anything important:

**The 60-Second Sanity Check:**

1. **Read the AI output critically, not passively.** Don't skim. Read it like a new employee wrote it and you're reviewing their work before it goes to a client — because that's essentially the situation.

2. **Flag any specific claims.** Numbers, dates, regulations, statistics, quotes, named sources — anything specific that could be verified or could be wrong.

3. **Verify the critical ones.** You don't need to verify every sentence. Focus on the claims that would cause real problems if they were wrong — financial figures, legal requirements, client-facing facts.

4. **Cross-reference with a second source.** Use Perplexity (which provides citations), a quick web search, or your own professional knowledge. If AI says a tax deadline is April 15th, you already know whether that's right. If AI says your state requires a specific license for a business activity, verify it.

5. **When in doubt, cut it.** If you can't verify a specific claim quickly, remove it or replace it with something you can confirm.

This isn't paranoia. It's the same professional standard you'd apply to any work product from a new hire. You wouldn't send a client letter drafted by a first-week employee without reading it. Treat AI output the same way.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** Never send an AI-written document to a client — especially anything involving numbers, deadlines, or legal requirements — without reading it out loud first. If it doesn't sound like you, it's not ready. If something sounds too specific and you can't verify it, take it out.

Even with the verification step, AI still saves substantial time. A 15-minute drafting task becomes 1 minute of AI generation plus 2 minutes of review — still a 12-minute savings. The verification step protects you; it doesn't eliminate the benefit.

### Data Privacy: What to Share and What to Guard

Every time you type something into an AI tool, you're sending that data to a server owned by the company that makes the tool. What happens to that data depends on which tool and which plan you're using.

**The critical rule: Never put confidential client data, proprietary business information, or personally identifiable information into a free-tier AI tool.**

Here's why. On free and most standard paid tiers, AI companies may use your inputs to improve their models. That means the information you type could, in theory, influence future outputs for other users. The chance of your specific data appearing verbatim in someone else's response is extremely low — but the principle matters, especially if you handle client financial data, health information, legal matters, or trade secrets.

**What's generally safe to share:**
- Generic business questions ("How should I structure an employee handbook?")
- Your own non-confidential writing that needs editing or improvement
- Publicly available information you want summarized or analyzed
- Hypothetical scenarios that don't use real names, numbers, or details
- General industry questions

**What you should never share on free/standard tiers:**
- Client names, account numbers, or financial details
- Employee Social Security numbers, salaries, or personal information
- Proprietary formulas, processes, or trade secrets
- Information covered by NDAs or confidentiality agreements
- Medical, legal, or financial records
- Passwords, credentials, or access codes

> **👉 AI Ron Recommends:** Use what I call the "coffee shop test" — imagine you're saying these words out loud at the table next to you in a coffee shop. If you'd lower your voice or turn the screen away, don't type it into a free AI tool. It's a simple gut check that works every time.

**The anonymization technique:** When you need AI's help with a task involving sensitive information, strip out the identifying details first. Instead of "Write a collection letter to John Smith at ABC Corp who owes us $47,500 from Invoice #3847," write "Write a collection letter to a commercial client who owes a significant past-due balance on an outstanding invoice." Add the real details back in after AI generates the draft.

### Client Confidentiality: The Non-Negotiable

If you're in a profession with confidentiality obligations — accounting, law, financial advising, healthcare, consulting — this section is especially for you.

Your professional obligations don't disappear because you're using a new tool. The same rules that prevent you from discussing client details at a dinner party apply when you're typing into ChatGPT. "But it's just an AI" is not a defense that works well with a licensing board.

**Practical guidelines:**
- Treat the AI chat window like a conversation in a public coffee shop. If you wouldn't say it out loud there, don't type it.
- If your work requires handling sensitive data with AI, enterprise-tier plans (ChatGPT Enterprise, Claude Team/Enterprise) contractually guarantee that your data isn't used for training and offer enhanced security controls. These cost more, but for regulated professions, they're worth it.
- Document your AI use policies. Even a one-page internal memo stating what can and can't be entered into AI tools is enough. Having it written down protects you.
- If a client asks whether you use AI, be honest. "Yes, I use AI tools for initial drafting, but all work is reviewed and finalized by me" is a perfectly respectable answer. Transparency builds trust. Awkward silence when the question comes up does not.

Note on enterprise pricing: Like everything else in AI, enterprise plan pricing and features are moving targets. ChatGPT Enterprise, Claude Team, and similar plans existed as of early 2026, but plan names, pricing, and data handling policies can change. Check directly with the provider for current terms — especially the data usage and privacy guarantees, which are the whole reason you'd pay for enterprise in the first place.

### The Compliance Lens

After 40+ years of working with businesses across multiple industries, I approach AI risk the same way I approach financial risk: with systems, not hopes.

**The CPA-trained approach to AI risk:**

1. **Materiality.** Not every AI error is equally dangerous. A typo in an internal brainstorming doc is trivial. An incorrect number in a client proposal is material. Focus your verification effort where the stakes are highest.

2. **Documentation.** Keep a lightweight record of how AI was used in important work products. You don't need a formal log. A note in your file — "Initial draft generated with Claude, reviewed and modified by [name] on [date]" — is sufficient and protects you if questions arise later.

3. **Segregation of duties.** Don't let AI be both the creator and the verifier. AI drafts. You (or a colleague) verify. This is the same principle that says the person who writes the checks shouldn't reconcile the bank statement. It applies here too.

4. **Regular review.** AI tools update frequently. Privacy policies change. Capabilities shift. Review your AI practices quarterly, the same way you'd review any business process with real stakes.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "The compliance mindset isn't about being paranoid — it's about being systematic. I've seen people lose clients over sloppy AI use the same way they'd lose them over sloppy work product. The tools changed; the professional standards didn't."

**Step 2:** Read the response carefully. It will sound authoritative and specific. That's the point.

**Step 3:** Verify the response. Go to IRS.gov or your state's tax authority website and check the key deadlines against what AI told you. Or ask Perplexity the same question and compare the cited sources.

**Step 4:** Note any discrepancies. Were the dates right? Were there deadlines AI missed? Did it confuse federal and state requirements?

**Step 5:** Write down what you found. You're not doing this to catch AI being wrong on this specific question (though you might). You're building the verification reflex — the habit that protects your business every time you use AI going forward.

The goal is to make verification automatic. Like checking your mirror before changing lanes. You do it every time, not just when you have a feeling something might be off.

**Estimated time: 15 minutes. ROI: Prevents errors that could cost hours of rework or, in regulated industries, significant penalties. The value is in what you avoid.**

## Key Takeaways
- AI hallucinations are not rare glitches — they're a fundamental feature of how these tools work. Verification isn't optional; it's professional.
- The 60-Second Sanity Check: read critically, flag specific claims, verify the ones that matter, cross-reference, and cut what you can't confirm
- Never put confidential client data, personal information, or proprietary details into free-tier AI tools. Use the anonymization technique for sensitive topics.
- Apply the compliance lens: materiality, documentation, segregation of duties, quarterly review — the same principles that keep your finances clean keep your AI use clean
