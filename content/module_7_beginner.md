## Business Template 1: Professional Email Draft

**When to use it:** Anytime you need to write a professional email and want it polished, clear, and appropriately toned — whether it's to a client, vendor, partner, or colleague. This is the one I use more than any other. If you only save one template from this entire module, save this one.

**The Template:**

```
Write a professional email with these details:

CONTEXT: I am a [your role] at [your company/business]. I'm writing to [recipient's name/role] at [their company]. Our relationship is [new contact / existing client / long-term partner / vendor].

ROLE: Write as a [friendly but professional / formal / warm and approachable] business communicator.

TASK: Draft an email that [states the purpose — requesting a meeting / following up on a proposal / introducing our services / confirming an agreement / etc.].

KEY POINTS TO INCLUDE:
- [Point 1]
- [Point 2]
- [Point 3]

TONE: [Professional and warm / Direct and concise / Formal and respectful]

LENGTH: Keep the email under [150/200/300] words.

FORMAT: Include a clear subject line, greeting, body, and sign-off.
```

**Example Filled In:**

```
Write a professional email with these details:

CONTEXT: I am the owner of Katz Financial Advisory, a CPA firm serving small businesses. I'm writing to Maria Chen, the owner of Chen's Bakery, a prospective client I met at the Chamber of Commerce mixer last week. Our relationship is new contact.

ROLE: Write as a friendly but professional financial advisor.

TASK: Draft an email that follows up on our conversation about her bookkeeping challenges and offers a free 30-minute consultation.

KEY POINTS TO INCLUDE:
- Reference our conversation about her QuickBooks frustrations
- Mention that we specialize in food service businesses
- Offer a free 30-minute consultation this week or next
- Keep it low-pressure — no hard sell

TONE: Warm and approachable, like a helpful neighbor who happens to be a CPA.

LENGTH: Keep the email under 200 words.

FORMAT: Include a clear subject line, greeting, body, and sign-off using my name, Ronnie Katz.
```

**Pro Tip:** Always specify the relationship with the recipient. An email to a new contact sounds very different from one to a 10-year client. The AI needs this context to get the tone right.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** Never send an AI-written email without reading it out loud first. If it doesn't sound like you, it's not ready. Your clients can tell the difference, even if they can't explain how.

## Business Template 2: Follow-Up Email

**When to use it:** After a meeting, proposal, event, or anytime someone hasn't responded and you need to nudge them without being annoying. We've all stared at a blank screen trying to figure out how to say "hey, did you forget about me?" without actually saying that. This template does it professionally.

**The Template:**

```
Write a follow-up email with these details:

CONTEXT: I am [your role] at [your company]. I'm following up with [recipient name/role] about [what you're following up on]. It has been [timeframe] since [our meeting / I sent the proposal / we last spoke / the event].

ORIGINAL INTERACTION: [Brief summary of what happened — what was discussed, what was promised, what's pending]

PURPOSE OF FOLLOW-UP: [Get a response to my proposal / Schedule the next meeting / Confirm they received my document / Check in on their decision timeline]

TONE: [Politely persistent / Casual check-in / Professionally direct]

IMPORTANT: Do NOT sound desperate, pushy, or passive-aggressive. Make it easy for them to respond with a simple yes/no or short answer.

LENGTH: Under [100/150] words. Short follow-ups get more responses.

FORMAT: Include a subject line that references our previous interaction.
```

**Example Filled In:**

```
Write a follow-up email with these details:

CONTEXT: I am the owner of Precision Plumbing Services. I'm following up with David Park, the facilities manager at Riverside Office Complex. It has been one week since I sent a proposal for their annual maintenance contract.

ORIGINAL INTERACTION: I met David on-site two weeks ago, walked the property, identified three priority areas (aging water heaters, lobby restroom fixtures, and break room plumbing). I sent a detailed proposal with three service tier options the following Monday.

PURPOSE OF FOLLOW-UP: Find out if he's had a chance to review the proposal, answer any questions, and understand his timeline for making a decision.

TONE: Politely persistent but not pushy. I want the contract but I don't want to seem desperate.

IMPORTANT: Do NOT sound desperate, pushy, or passive-aggressive. Make it easy for him to respond with a simple yes/no or short answer.

LENGTH: Under 120 words.

FORMAT: Include a subject line that references the Riverside Office Complex proposal.
```

**Pro Tip:** Tell the AI "make it easy for them to respond." This produces emails that end with a simple question or a yes/no option, which dramatically increases response rates.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "The number-one mistake in follow-up emails is making the recipient do work. 'Let me know your thoughts' requires them to organize their thoughts. 'Does Thursday at 2pm work, or would a different time be better?' requires them to check a calendar. One of those is easier to answer."

## Business Template 3: Difficult Conversation Email

**When to use it:** When you need to deliver bad news, address a problem, push back on something, or handle a sensitive situation — and you want to get the words exactly right. Firing a client. Raising prices. Saying no. AI handles the first draft so you don't spend two hours staring at a blinking cursor, feeling terrible.

**The Template:**

```
Write a difficult but professional email with these details:

CONTEXT: I am [your role] at [your company]. I need to communicate with [recipient name/role] about [the difficult topic].

THE SITUATION: [Explain what happened — be specific. What went wrong, what changed, what's the problem?]

WHAT I NEED TO COMMUNICATE:
- [The core message — price increase / service termination / boundary setting / error acknowledgment / disagreement]
- [Any explanation or context they need]
- [What happens next or what I'm asking them to do]

RELATIONSHIP CONSIDERATIONS: [How important is this relationship? Am I willing to negotiate? Do I want to preserve the relationship or is this a firm boundary?]

TONE: [Firm but respectful / Empathetic but clear / Direct but not harsh / Apologetic but not groveling]

WHAT TO AVOID:
- Don't blame or point fingers
- Don't over-apologize or sound weak
- Don't leave room for ambiguity about [the decision / the boundary / the next step]

LENGTH: [200-300] words. Be thorough enough to be clear but don't ramble.

FORMAT: Include a clear subject line. Structure the email as: acknowledge the situation, explain the decision, state next steps.
```

**Pro Tip:** For truly sensitive emails, always add: "Give me three versions — one that's slightly warmer, one that's more direct, and one in the middle. I'll pick the one that feels right." Then you can mix and match the best parts.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** Difficult emails get better when you sleep on the draft. Have AI write it. Read it the next morning. What felt necessary at 8pm often feels excessive at 8am. The edit is where the wisdom lives.

## Business Template 4: Meeting Recap / Action Items

**When to use it:** After any meeting, call, or important conversation where you need to document what was discussed and who's doing what by when. You know the feeling — meeting ends, you have a page of chicken-scratch notes, and now you have to turn it into something people can actually follow. Hand the chicken-scratch to AI. It loves this kind of mess.

**The Template:**

```
Create a professional meeting recap email based on these notes:

MEETING DETAILS:
- Meeting: [name/purpose of meeting]
- Date: [date]
- Attendees: [list of people]
- Duration: [how long]

MY RAW NOTES (clean these up — they're messy):
[Paste your actual messy meeting notes here — bullet points, fragments, shorthand — whatever you jotted down]

FORMAT THE RECAP AS:
1. Meeting Summary (2-3 sentences of what was discussed overall)
2. Key Decisions Made (bullet points of anything that was decided)
3. Action Items (formatted as: [Person responsible] — [What they need to do] — [By when])
4. Open Questions / Parking Lot (anything that still needs to be resolved)
5. Next Meeting: [date/time if applicable]

TONE: Professional but not stiff. This is an internal recap, not a legal document.

IMPORTANT: If my notes are unclear about who owns an action item or what the deadline is, flag it with [CONFIRM] so I can fill it in before sending.
```

**Pro Tip:** Don't clean up your notes before pasting them in. The AI is excellent at turning chaos into order. The messier your notes, the more time the AI saves you. Just dump everything in.

## Business Template 5: Social Media Post (Platform-Specific)

**When to use it:** When you need to create a social media post and want it optimized for a specific platform's style, length, and audience expectations. Here's something most people get wrong: they write one post and throw it on every platform. LinkedIn loves stories and professional lessons. Instagram wants a visual hook. X wants punchy and brief. They're three completely different conversations. This template handles all three.

### LinkedIn Variant:

```
Write a LinkedIn post for my business with these details:

CONTEXT: I am [your role] at [your company]. My LinkedIn audience is [describe your connections — other business owners / industry professionals / potential clients / mix].

TOPIC: [What the post is about — a lesson learned, industry insight, company news, opinion, tip]

KEY MESSAGE: [The one thing you want people to take away from this post]

STORY OR HOOK: [Any specific story, example, or experience to build the post around. If you don't have one, say "create a relatable hook."]

CALL TO ACTION: [What do you want people to do? Comment, share, visit your website, DM you, just think about something differently?]

STYLE GUIDELINES:
- Open with a strong first line that makes people click "see more"
- Use short paragraphs (1-2 sentences each)
- Include line breaks for readability
- Keep it under 1,300 characters (the sweet spot before LinkedIn truncates)
- Sound like a real person, not a corporate press release
- No hashtag spam — 3-5 relevant hashtags maximum, placed at the end
```

### Instagram Variant:

```
Write an Instagram caption for my business with these details:

CONTEXT: I am [your role] at [your company]. My Instagram audience is [describe your followers].

THE IMAGE/VIDEO I'M POSTING: [Describe what the visual content shows — even if you haven't created it yet, describe what it will be]

KEY MESSAGE: [What you want people to take away]

CALL TO ACTION: [Comment a specific emoji, tag a friend, save this post, click link in bio, share to stories]

STYLE GUIDELINES:
- First line must hook — this shows in the feed before people tap "more"
- Write in a conversational, authentic tone
- Use paragraph breaks for readability
- Keep the main caption under 150 words (people scroll fast)
- Put hashtags in a separate comment block (I'll copy them separately) — give me 20-25 relevant hashtags mixing popular, medium, and niche
- Include 1-2 relevant emojis if they fit naturally — don't overdo it
```

### X/Twitter Variant:

```
Write a post for X/Twitter for my business with these details:

CONTEXT: I am [your role] at [your company].

TOPIC: [What this post is about]

KEY MESSAGE: [The one thing to communicate]

FORMAT: [Single post under 280 characters / Thread of 3-5 posts / Quote-tweet style commentary]

STYLE GUIDELINES:
- Be punchy and direct — every word must earn its place
- If writing a thread, make the first post strong enough to stand alone
- Sound like a smart human having a conversation, not a brand broadcasting
- No more than 1-2 hashtags (X users find hashtag-heavy posts spammy)
- If a thread, number each post (1/5, 2/5, etc.)
```

**Pro Tip:** Always write the same core message differently for each platform. A LinkedIn post that kills it will flop on Instagram. Tell the AI the platform first, and it automatically adjusts tone, length, and style.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "I resisted social media for years. Thought it was for people with too much time and too little to say. Then I watched a competitor get three new clients from a single LinkedIn post that took him 10 minutes to write with AI. That cured my skepticism fast."

## Business Template 6: Blog Post Outline

**When to use it:** When you want to plan a blog post before writing it — or when you want to give direction to a writer (human or AI). The outline is where good blog posts get made or ruined. Spend 10 minutes on a strong outline and you'll save an hour on the draft.

**The Template:**

```
Create a detailed blog post outline on this topic:

CONTEXT: I run [your business] and my blog audience is [who reads your blog — potential clients, existing customers, industry peers].

TOPIC: [What the blog post is about]

GOAL: [What this post should accomplish — drive traffic, establish authority, generate leads, educate customers, rank for a keyword]

TARGET KEYWORD (if applicable): [The search term you want to rank for]

READER'S PROBLEM: [What question or challenge brings someone to this post?]

OUTLINE SHOULD INCLUDE:
1. A compelling headline (give me 3 options)
2. Introduction hook (the first 2-3 sentences that grab attention)
3. 5-7 main sections with:
   - Section heading
   - 2-3 bullet points of what to cover in each section
   - Any data, examples, or stories to include
4. Conclusion with clear takeaway
5. Call to action (what should the reader do next?)
6. Meta description (under 160 characters for SEO)

TARGET LENGTH: [800-1000 words / 1200-1500 words / 2000+ words]

TONE: [Educational / Conversational / Authoritative / Friendly and approachable]
```

**Pro Tip:** Ask for 3 headline options. Headlines make or break blog posts, and having choices lets you pick the one that best matches your voice and audience.

## Business Template 7: Marketing Email / Newsletter

**When to use it:** For promotional emails, newsletters, or any email sent to your customer/subscriber list. Your email list is arguably your most valuable business asset. People gave you their address. They let you in. Treat the inbox like a guest in someone's home — show up with something worth their time, not just another pitch.

**The Template:**

```
Write a marketing email/newsletter with these details:

CONTEXT: My business is [your business]. This email is going to [describe your email list — existing customers, prospects, newsletter subscribers, etc.].

EMAIL TYPE: [Promotional / Newsletter / Nurture / Re-engagement / Announcement / Educational]

GOAL: [What do you want people to DO after reading this? Buy something, click a link, reply, attend an event, just stay engaged?]

SUBJECT LINE: Give me 5 options. [Note any constraints — keep it under 50 characters, avoid spam trigger words, etc.]

PREVIEW TEXT: Write preview text that complements (not repeats) each subject line.

MAIN CONTENT:
- Key message: [what you're communicating]
- Supporting points: [2-3 things that reinforce the key message]
- Offer or value: [discount, free resource, exclusive content, invitation — whatever you're giving them]
- Urgency (if real): [deadline, limited spots, seasonal relevance — ONLY if genuine]

CTA: [One clear primary action — what button should they click?]

TONE: [Friendly and personal / Professional / Excited but not salesy / Casual like an email from a friend]

LENGTH: [Under 200 words for promotional / 300-500 for newsletter / Under 150 for re-engagement]

IMPORTANT: Write like a human, not a marketing machine. My readers are real people who get 100 emails a day. This needs to feel like it came from a real person who actually cares.
```

**Pro Tip:** Always ask for 5 subject line options. The subject line determines whether your email gets opened or deleted, and having options lets you A/B test or pick the best one for your audience.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** Never fake urgency. "Only 3 left!!!" when you have 300 in stock is the kind of thing people notice. Once they catch you, trust is gone and it never fully comes back. If the deadline is real, say so. If it's not, don't invent one.

## Business Template 8: Document / Report Summary

**When to use it:** When you have a long document, report, article, or email thread and need the key points extracted quickly — perfect for contracts, reports, meeting transcripts, or research. Forty-year-old eyes, twelve-page lease renewal. You know where I'm going with this.

**The Template:**

```
Summarize the following document/report:

DOCUMENT TYPE: [Contract / Financial report / Research paper / Meeting transcript / Email thread / Industry report / Policy document]

MY ROLE: I am [your role] and I'm reading this because [why — to make a decision, to prepare for a meeting, to understand implications for my business, etc.]

WHAT I NEED FROM THIS SUMMARY:
1. Key findings/points (the 5-7 most important things)
2. Decisions or actions required (anything I need to do or decide)
3. Risks or concerns (anything that should worry me)
4. Opportunities (anything positive I should act on)
5. Numbers that matter (key figures, dates, deadlines, financial data)

FORMAT: Bullet points, organized by the five categories above. Put the most important/urgent items first in each category.

LENGTH: [Keep the entire summary under 500 words / Give me a one-paragraph executive summary plus the detailed breakdown]

IMPORTANT: If anything is ambiguous or could be interpreted multiple ways, flag it. If there's anything a reasonable person might miss on a quick read, highlight it.

THE DOCUMENT:
[Paste the document text here]
```

**Pro Tip:** Always tell the AI your role and WHY you're reading the document. A CEO reading a financial report needs different highlights than an accountant reading the same report. Context changes everything.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** AI can summarize a contract beautifully. It cannot replace an attorney reviewing it. Use AI to understand what you're looking at and identify the questions to ask your lawyer. That's how you get smart AND protected.

## Business Template 9: Customer Service Response

**When to use it:** When you need to respond to a customer complaint, question, or request — especially when emotions are involved and you want to get the tone exactly right. The angry customer email at 10pm when you're irritated. Whatever you're about to type is going to be either too defensive or too apologetic. Hand it to AI first. Let it take the emotion out of it.

**The Template:**

```
Write a customer service response with these details:

CONTEXT: My business is [your business type]. The customer is [name if applicable] and they are a [new customer / loyal customer of X years / VIP / first-time buyer].

THE SITUATION: [What happened — the complaint, question, or issue. Be specific about what went wrong and how the customer communicated it (angry email, calm phone call, public review, etc.)]

THE FACTS: [What actually happened on your end — was it your fault, a misunderstanding, a third-party issue, an unreasonable expectation?]

WHAT I CAN OFFER:
- [Refund / Partial refund / Credit / Free service / Replacement / Nothing — explain why]
- [Any additional gesture — discount on next purchase, free upgrade, personal follow-up]

WHAT I CANNOT DO: [Any limitations — can't refund past 30 days, can't replace a custom order, policy restrictions]

RESPONSE FORMAT: [Email / Review response (public) / Chat message / Letter]

TONE: [Empathetic and solutions-focused / Professional and firm / Warm and apologetic / Calm and factual]

IMPORTANT:
- Acknowledge their frustration WITHOUT admitting fault for things that aren't our fault
- Focus on the solution, not the problem
- Don't over-promise or make commitments I can't keep
- If this is a public response (review), remember that potential customers will read this too
```

**Pro Tip:** For public responses (reviews), always add: "Remember that my response is really for the hundreds of future customers who will read this, not just the one person who wrote it. Show that we're reasonable, responsive, and fair."

## Business Template 10: Decision Analysis (Pros/Cons with Recommendation)

**When to use it:** When you're facing a business decision and want a structured analysis to help you think through it clearly. In 40 years of business, I've seen more bad decisions made confidently than good decisions made carefully. This template forces the careful part.

**The Template:**

```
Help me analyze this business decision:

THE DECISION: [What you're trying to decide — be specific]

CONTEXT: [Background information — your business, current situation, what led to this decision point]

THE OPTIONS:
- Option A: [Describe option A]
- Option B: [Describe option B]
- Option C: [Describe option C, if applicable]
(Include as many options as you're considering, including "do nothing / stay the course")

FACTORS THAT MATTER MOST TO ME:
1. [Factor 1 — e.g., cost, risk, time, revenue impact, team morale, customer experience]
2. [Factor 2]
3. [Factor 3]

MY CONSTRAINTS:
- Budget: [What can you afford?]
- Timeline: [When do you need to decide? When does it need to be implemented?]
- Risk tolerance: [Low — I can't afford a mistake / Medium — some risk is OK / High — I'm willing to bet big]

FORMAT:
1. Summary of each option (2-3 sentences)
2. Pros and cons for each option (bullet points)
3. Risk assessment for each option (what could go wrong, how likely, how bad)
4. Recommended option with clear reasoning

IMPORTANT: Challenge my thinking. If I'm clearly leaning toward one option, push back and make sure I've considered the downsides. I want an honest analysis, not confirmation of what I already think.
```

**Pro Tip:** Always include a "do nothing" option. It forces you (and the AI) to weigh the cost of inaction, which is often the most overlooked risk in business decisions.

## Business Template 11: Job Posting / Description

**When to use it:** When you need to hire someone and want a job posting that attracts the right candidates while clearly communicating expectations. The worst job postings use so many buzzwords they say nothing. The best ones are honest, specific, and make a great candidate think "that sounds like exactly where I want to work."

**The Template:**

```
Write a job posting with these details:

COMPANY: [Your business name, what you do, your size, your culture in a few words]

POSITION: [Job title]

WHY WE'RE HIRING: [New position due to growth / Replacement / Expanding the team]

THE ROLE IN PLAIN ENGLISH: [Describe what this person will actually DO day-to-day — not corporate HR language, but "here's what a typical week looks like"]

MUST-HAVE QUALIFICATIONS:
- [Skill/experience 1]
- [Skill/experience 2]
- [Skill/experience 3]
(Keep this to genuine must-haves — things you'd reject a candidate for lacking)

NICE-TO-HAVE QUALIFICATIONS:
- [Bonus skill 1]
- [Bonus skill 2]

COMPENSATION:
- Salary range: [Be transparent — include the range]
- Benefits: [List what you offer — health, PTO, retirement, remote work, etc.]

POSTING FORMAT:
1. Opening hook (2-3 sentences that make someone stop scrolling)
2. About us (brief company description)
3. What you'll do (responsibilities)
4. What you bring (qualifications)
5. What we offer (compensation and benefits)
6. How to apply

TONE: [Professional but human / Casual and fun / Warm and welcoming / Direct and no-nonsense]

IMPORTANT: Don't use jargon like "synergy," "rockstar," "ninja," or "wear many hats." Write like a real person describing a real job to a smart adult.
```

**Pro Tip:** Include the salary range. Job postings with salary ranges get 30% more applicants, and the applicants are better-matched because they self-select. Hiding the salary wastes everyone's time.

> **🗣️ AI Ron Says:** "I've hired a lot of people over the years. The best ones came when I wrote job postings that were honest about the hard parts — not just the good parts. People self-select out when they know what they're signing up for. That saves everyone months of misery."

## Business Template 12: Product Description

**When to use it:** When you need to write product descriptions for your website, store, or catalog that actually make people want to buy. Most product descriptions are lists of features. The best ones describe what life looks like after someone buys the thing.

**The Template:**

```
Write a product description with these details:

THE PRODUCT: [Product name and what it is]

TARGET CUSTOMER: [Who buys this — age, interests, what problem they're solving, what they care about]

KEY FEATURES:
- [Feature 1]
- [Feature 2]
- [Feature 3]
- [Feature 4]

KEY BENEFITS: [What does each feature actually DO for the customer? Why should they care?]

PRICE POINT: $[price] — position as [budget-friendly / mid-range / premium / luxury]

COMPETITOR COMPARISON: [What makes this different from alternatives? What's the unique selling point?]

FORMAT:
- Attention-grabbing headline
- 2-3 sentence opening that speaks to the customer's need or desire
- Feature/benefit breakdown (bullet points or short paragraphs)
- One line addressing the most common objection
- Call to action

TONE: [Conversational and warm / Premium and aspirational / Practical and no-nonsense / Fun and energetic]

LENGTH: [50-75 words for quick listing / 150-200 words for detailed description / 300+ for full product page]

IMPORTANT: Focus on benefits, not just features. "500 lumens" means nothing to most people. "Bright enough to light your entire backyard for the summer cookout" paints a picture.
```

**Pro Tip:** After AI drafts the description, ask: "Now rewrite this so it answers the question: 'Why should I buy THIS instead of the cheaper one on Amazon?'" That question forces the description to sell, not just describe.

## Personal Template 1: Travel Itinerary Planner

**When to use it:** When you're planning a trip and want a thoughtful, detailed itinerary — whether it's a weekend getaway or a two-week international trip. You spend 10 minutes filling in the template and get back a day-by-day plan that would take a travel agent hours to produce.

**The Template:**

```
Plan a travel itinerary with these details:

DESTINATION: [Where you're going]
DATES: [Travel dates]
TRAVELERS: [Who's going — ages, interests, mobility considerations]
BUDGET: [Total trip budget OR daily budget OR "flexible" / "budget-conscious" / "luxury"]
```

# SECTION 7.4: THE AI OUTPUT QUALITY CHECKLIST — The ATCSA System

*Here's the uncomfortable truth: AI can write something that looks perfect and is subtly wrong. Fluent, confident, and wrong. The difference between people who succeed with AI and people who get embarrassed by it is a 2-minute review.*

---

## Meet ATCSA: Your 5-Point Quality Check

Before using ANY AI-generated content, run it through these five checkpoints. Think of it like proofreading, but broader — you're checking not just for typos but for the stuff that could actually get you in trouble.

The acronym is **ATCSA**: **Accuracy, Tone, Completeness, Sensitivity, Action.**

Here's what each one means:

### A — ACCURACY

**The question:** Are the facts, numbers, names, dates, and claims correct?

AI is confident even when it's wrong. It doesn't hedge — it presents made-up statistics with the same confidence as real ones. So you need to verify anything specific.

**Quick checks:**
- Any specific statistic or number → verify with a quick search
- Names and titles → make sure they're spelled right and current
- Legal or regulatory references → confirm they actually exist and are current
- If it cites a study or source → verify it's real (AI sometimes invents citations)

**Example of failure:** AI writes "According to IRS Publication 587, home office deductions require a dedicated room." WRONG — the rules actually require a space that's "regularly and exclusively used," not necessarily a separate room. One wrong sentence in a tax advisory email could cost your client money and cost you your credibility.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** AI is confident even when it's wrong. It doesn't know what it doesn't know. Always verify specific facts, numbers, and regulatory references. Always. No exceptions.

---

### T — TONE

**The question:** Does this sound like you? Is it appropriate for the audience and situation?

**Quick checks:**
- Read it out loud — would you actually say these words?
- Check for language that's too formal, too casual, or too corporate for the context
- Watch for AI-typical phrases: "I hope this email finds you well," "In today's rapidly evolving landscape," "Harness the power of"
- Ask yourself: "If the recipient knows me, would this sound like me?"

**Example of failure:** AI writes a "fun and casual" product recall notice. Product recalls require serious, clear, factual communication. Wrong tone can create legal liability.

---

### C — COMPLETENESS

**The question:** Does this cover everything it needs to? Is anything important missing?

**Quick checks:**
- Does it answer the actual question or address the real purpose?
- Are all the points you wanted to make included?
- Is there a clear call to action or next step?
- Are there obvious follow-up questions a reader would have?

**Example of failure:** AI writes a client proposal that describes the solution beautifully but never mentions pricing, timeline, or next steps. The client has to email back asking basic questions — making you look unprepared.

---

### S — SENSITIVITY

**The question:** Could this content offend, exclude, alienate, or cause harm?

**Quick checks:**
- Read it from the perspective of your most sensitive stakeholder
- Check for assumptions about gender, age, race, religion, ability, or socioeconomic status
- For public content, imagine the worst possible interpretation — could someone screenshot this and make it look bad?

**Example of failure:** AI generates a marketing email for a Mother's Day promotion that says "Because every mom deserves..." Sounds fine until you consider subscribers who've lost their mothers, can't have children, or have difficult relationships with their mothers. A simple reframe to "Celebrate the special women in your life" avoids the landmine.

---

### A — ACTION

**The question:** Is it clear what should happen next? Is this content actually usable?

**Quick checks:**
- Is there a clear call to action or next step?
- Can the reader do what you're asking them to do? (Is the link included? Is the deadline clear?)
- Is this ready to send/publish, or does it need additional components?
- Search for "[" — if you find brackets, you've got unfilled template fields

**Example of failure:** AI writes a beautiful event invitation but says "Click here to RSVP" without an actual link. The reader wants to act but can't.

---

## The 30-Second Version

Don't have time for the full checklist? Here's the quick version:

1. **Accurate?** — Scan for any specific facts or numbers. Verify anything you're not 100% sure about.
2. **Tone?** — Read it out loud. Does it sound like you?
3. **Complete?** — Is anything important missing? Is there a clear next step?
4. **Sensitive?** — Could this upset or exclude anyone?
5. **Actionable?** — Is it clear what happens next? Are all the blanks filled in?

Two minutes. Five checks. Every time.

---

## How Deep Should Your Review Go?

Not everything needs the same level of scrutiny:

| What you're sending | Review level | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Internal notes, personal reminders, casual messages | Quick scan | 30 seconds |
| Client emails, social media, blog posts, proposals | Full ATCSA check + read aloud | 2-5 minutes |
| Legal documents, financial reports, policy documents, press releases | Full ATCSA check × 2. Fact-check every claim. Have someone else read it. | 10-15 minutes |

---

## The "Would I Put My Name On This?" Test

Before sending any AI-generated content, ask yourself:

1. If this content had my name at the top and went viral tomorrow, would I be proud or embarrassed?
2. If my most important client received this, would they think more or less of me?
3. If my toughest competitor read this, would they find anything to use against me?

If the answer to any of these is "no" or "I'm not sure," the content needs more work.

# SECTION 7.6: YOUR BUSINESS AI POLICY — Why You Need One

*Most businesses have no policy on AI use. Which means every employee is improvising — some brilliantly, some recklessly, and most somewhere in between.*

---

## Why This Matters (Even If You're a One-Person Shop)

You might be thinking: "I don't have a team. Why do I need a policy?" Fair question. Here's why:

**If you have employees (even one):** You need clear guidelines on what data can and can't go into AI tools. The day someone pastes a client's Social Security number into ChatGPT is the day you realize you should have had this conversation sooner.

**If it's just you:** A personal AI policy is like a checklist for a pilot. Even experienced pilots use them because when you're moving fast, you forget things. A simple set of rules keeps you from accidentally putting sensitive information where it doesn't belong.

## What Your AI Policy Should Cover

At minimum, any business AI policy needs these five things:

1. **Approved tools** — Which AI tools are people allowed to use for work?
2. **Data rules** — What information can and absolutely CANNOT go into AI tools? (Client names, financials, SSNs, health info — all off limits.)
3. **Review requirements** — What level of human review is required before AI output goes to a client or goes public?
4. **Disclosure guidelines** — When should you tell clients or customers that AI was involved?
5. **Incident plan** — What happens if something goes wrong? (Wrong info sent to a client, data entered where it shouldn't have been.)

## The Golden Rule of AI Data

Here's the simplest version of your data policy:

**If you wouldn't post it on a public bulletin board, don't paste it into an AI tool.**

That covers about 90% of the situations you'll face. When in doubt, anonymize. Replace real names with fake ones. Remove account numbers. Generalize specifics. Then paste it in.

**Example:**

❌ WRONG: "Write a collection letter to John Martinez at 123 Oak St, Austin TX for his overdue balance of $4,750 on invoice #4892."

✅ RIGHT: "Write a professional collection letter to a client who has an overdue balance of approximately $5,000, 60 days past due. Tone should be firm but maintain the relationship."

Same quality output. Zero privacy risk.

> **⚖️ AI Ron's Rule:** A policy that nobody reads is just a legal document. Make this readable. The goal isn't to scare people — it's to make sure everyone's playing by the same rules before someone accidentally puts a client's social security number into ChatGPT.

### Answer Key

1. **b** — Accuracy first. A beautifully toned email with wrong facts is worse than a rough email with correct facts. Always verify specific claims before anything else.

2. **c** — Start free, upgrade when you hit limits. Every major tool has a free tier. Use it, learn it, and only pay when you know it's worth it. Buying everything upfront is how you end up with subscriptions you forget about.

3. **b** — Sensitivity. This is the check that catches the landmines — assumptions, exclusions, or content that could be misinterpreted. It's the one most people skip, and the one that causes the most damage when missed.

4. **c** — Make it easy to respond. Ending with a simple question or yes/no option dramatically increases response rates. Long, open-ended emails get filed in the "I'll get to this later" pile — which means never.

5. **b** — Assessment first. Track your time, find the opportunities, pick ONE project. The people who skip assessment and jump to "AI ALL THE THINGS" are the ones who burn out by Week 3.

6. **c** — The anonymized version. Same quality output, zero privacy risk. Never put identifying client information into AI tools. Anonymize first, always.

7. **c** — Search for "[". This catches unfilled template brackets like [Your Name] or [Insert Date]. It happens more than you'd think — and sending an email that says "Dear [Client Name]" is... not a great look.

8. **b** — Perplexity is purpose-built for research with real-time sources. ChatGPT can browse the web, but Perplexity was designed from the ground up to find, cite, and verify information. Different tools, different strengths.
